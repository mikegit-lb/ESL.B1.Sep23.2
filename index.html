Text 1 · Lesson Reading

🐱

Merhaba! I'm Kedi, your detective sidekick for today. Spoiler: I already know who did it. I'm not telling. Read the case file. 🔍

I Accused My Best Friend — Then I Remembered

A missing trophy, three suspects, and a memory that changed everything.

Past Simple — the main story, one event after another Past Perfect — something that happened even earlier

The Trophy That Disappeared

On Friday afternoon, the school's biggest trophy vanished from the display case. Ms. Ortiz walked into the hall and found the case empty. Someone had broken the lock two days earlier, and nobody had noticed until now. It was, by every measure, a total mystery.

My Prime Suspect

I immediately thought of Jonas. He had been acting suspicious all week — skipping lunch, avoiding eye contact, disappearing during breaks. When I confronted him in the hallway, he turned pale and admitted he had been hiding something. My stomach dropped. I was sure I had caught the thief — and honestly, I felt a little guilty for enjoying it.

The Twist

Jonas hadn't stolen anything. Turns out, he had been secretly rehearsing a magic trick for the talent show — using a trophy-shaped prop he had borrowed from the drama department. The real evidence? I found it myself, in a box I had moved to the storage room the week before, when I had helped Ms. Ortiz clean the display case. I had forgotten completely. In the end, I apologized to Jonas about seventeen times, and he still brings it up.

So — have you ever accused the wrong person, or gotten blamed for something you didn't do? Tell me the story out loud — you'll need both tenses to make it land.

🐱

Real talk: Jonas practicing a magic trick with a fake trophy is the most "theatre kid" plot twist I've ever heard. 10/10, no notes.

Vocabulary Bank

30 words for telling a good twist

Say each word out loud once before we move on. Some of these you already half-know — trust that instinct.

clue
a piece of information that helps solve a mystery
"The open window was the first clue."
accuse
to say someone did something wrong
"Don't accuse me before you have proof!"
secret
something you don't tell other people
"She kept the secret for years."
suspicious
making you feel something bad is happening
"His smile looked suspicious."
disappear
to go somewhere nobody can see you
"The cat disappeared under the bed."
confront
to talk to someone directly about a problem
"I had to confront him about the note."
guilty
feeling bad because you did something wrong
"He looked guilty the moment I asked."
coincidence
when two things happen together by chance
"What a coincidence — we wore the same shirt!"
nervous
worried or anxious about something
"I was nervous before the exam."
evidence
facts or objects that prove something is true
"The muddy shoes were strong evidence."
honest
telling the truth, not hiding anything
"Just be honest with me."
conceal
to hide something on purpose
"She tried to conceal her disappointment."
forgive
to stop being angry at someone
"I forgave him after he explained."
contradict
to say the opposite of what someone said
"Her story contradicted his completely."
embarrassed
feeling shy or awkward after a mistake
"I was so embarrassed I laughed instead."
alibi
proof that you were somewhere else
"Luckily, I had a solid alibi."
mistake
something done wrong, usually by accident
"Blaming Jonas was a huge mistake."
testify
to formally say what you saw, as proof
"She testified that she saw nothing."
admit
to say something is true, even if it's hard
"I had to admit I was wrong."
assumption
something you believe without checking it's true
"That was a dangerous assumption to make."
surprise
something unexpected
"The ending was a total surprise."
rehearse
to practice before the real performance
"He'd been rehearsing the trick for weeks."
innocent
not guilty — did nothing wrong
"He was innocent the whole time."
misunderstanding
when people don't understand each other correctly
"The whole thing was one big misunderstanding."
promise
to say you will definitely do something
"I promise I'll listen first next time."
overreact
to respond too strongly to something small
"I definitely overreacted about the trophy."
discover
to find or learn something for the first time
"I discovered the trophy in my own box."
backfire
when a plan causes the opposite of what you wanted
"My detective plan completely backfired."
relief
the good feeling when worry stops
"Finding the trophy was pure relief."
plausible

"His magic-trick excuse actually sounded plausible."

You'll hear several of these again in Text 2, the Tense Detective quiz, and the speaking activities — so you don't need to memorize the list, just recognize it when it shows up.

Text 2 · Lesson Reading

The Note in My Locker

A short, twisty read for extra tense practice — same rules, new mystery.

I opened my locker on Monday morning and found a folded note I hadn't put there. It said: "I know what you did." My stomach dropped. Who had written it? I hadn't done anything wrong — at least, nothing I could think of. All day, I felt nervous, replaying every conversation I'd had that week, searching for a clue. By lunch, I had convinced myself that half the school suspected me of something.

I confronted my friend Elif, since she'd been acting oddly distant. She looked confused, then laughed so hard she nearly cried. Turns out, she'd written the note for a completely different Aylin — there are three Aylins in our year — and she'd put it in the wrong locker by mistake. I felt relief, then embarrassed, then relief again.

Lesson learned: not every mystery is about you. Sometimes it's just a mix-up with someone else's homework drama.

Grammar Contrast

Look at these pairs from the article

Past Simple

“The trophy vanished.”
“I confronted him.”
“I found it myself.”

Past Perfect

“Someone had broken the lock.”
“He had been hiding something.”
“I had moved it the week before.”

Q1Which tense tells the story in the order it happened — one event, then the next?
Q2When Ms. Ortiz found the case empty, had the lock already been broken, or did it break at that exact moment?
Q3Why does the writer say “I had moved it the week before” instead of just “I moved it”? What does “had” add?
Complete the rule: We use past simple for the events of the story, told in order. We use + (past participle) for something that had already happened before that story starts — the hidden backstory behind the twist.
Show answer key

Q1: Past simple.   Q2: It had already been broken — that's why past perfect is used.   Q3: “Had” shows the moving happened before the main story's events, not at the same time.   Rule: had + past participle.

🇹🇷 Cheat code you already own: Turkish already has this exact idea — the -mıştı form. “Ödevimi çoktan yapmıştım” means exactly “I had already done my homework.” You've been thinking in past perfect your whole life. English just makes you use two words instead of one suffix. Rude, but fair.
yapmıştım → had done · gitmiştim → had gone · unutmuştum → had forgotten

Tense Detective

Identify the tense — then check yourself

Read each line out loud. Say Past Simple or Past Perfect, then click Reveal. Get it right and Kedi celebrates. Get it wrong and Kedi also celebrates, just more sarcastically.

“Ms. Ortiz walked into the hall.”
Past Simple — one action in the main timeline.🎉 Correct-ish energy. Ms. Ortiz would be proud.
“He had been acting weird all week.”
Past Perfect — background true before the confrontation.👀 Suspicious grammar for a suspicious guy.
“She'd been acting oddly distant.”
Past Perfect — from Text 2, background before the confrontation.🕵️ Three Aylins in one year is genuinely chaotic.
“He had borrowed the prop from the drama department.”
Past Perfect — explains the twist.🎭 Plot twist: he was just theatre kid-ing.
“I apologized to Jonas about seventeen times.”
Past Simple — the final event that closes the story.😅 Seventeen is a lot, but honestly, fair.

Speaking Studio — 5 Activities

🐱

No hiding behind a group today. It's you, me, and one very judgmental cat. Let's talk.

1 · Warm-Up

Two Truths and a Lie (Past Perfect Edition)

Light and fast — gets your voice moving before the harder activities.

Tell me three things that had already happened before you turned 10. Two are true, one is a lie. I have to guess.

  • Example “By the time I was 10, I had already broken my arm twice, I had never eaten a vegetable, and I had already visited three countries.”
2 · Roleplay

The Interrogation (We Swap Roles)

Fun and thought-provoking — you have to build a believable story on the spot.

Round 1: I'm the Detective, you're the Suspect. Round 2: we swap — you interrogate me about a "crime" I clearly did not commit (I'm innocent, I swear). Detective asks what happened (past simple); Suspect must explain what had already happened before that (past perfect) to survive.

Detective asks
  • Where were you when it disappeared?
  • What did you do right before that?
  • Why were you acting so strange?
Suspect must explain
  • Something you had already done earlier
  • A reason you had been avoiding people
  • A secret you had been keeping — unrelated to the "crime"
  • Detective: “So what happened exactly?”
  • Suspect: “Well, before that, I had already…”
  • Suspect: “I was acting strange because I had been…”
3 · Deduction Game

Suspect Line-Up

Fun and vocabulary-heavy — you build a case using the word bank, I try to guess wrong on purpose.

Pick one "suspect" below without telling me which. Describe them using at least 4 vocabulary words and both tenses — what they did, and what they'd already done before that made them look suspicious. I guess who it is. Then we switch: I describe, you guess.

🎭
The Drama Kid
had been rehearsing all week
📚
The Quiet Genius
had already finished every exam early
⚽
The Team Captain
had promised to keep a secret
  • Example: “This person had been acting suspicious, and everyone assumed they were guilty — but they had a plausible alibi.”
4 · Storytelling

Twist Retell

Fun and creative — same facts, new narrator, new drama.

Retell the whole trophy story to me from Jonas's point of view. Keep the twist, but this time we hear what he had been secretly planning all along, before the accusation happened. Make it dramatic. Kedi is judging your storytelling skills.

  • Main events (past simple): “On Friday, my friend accused me…”
  • Backstory (past perfect): “What she didn't know was that I had…”
5 · Discussion

The Verdict

Thought-provoking — no right answer, just your honest opinion, in English.

This one isn't a game. I want your real opinion, explained in full sentences.

  • Was the narrator right to suspect Jonas before she had any real evidence — or was that an unfair assumption?
  • Jonas never explained himself until he was confronted. Should he have told the truth earlier, or was hiding a talent-show surprise okay?
  • Have you ever assumed something about a friend that turned out to be completely wrong? What had actually happened?

Self-check before you speak

Blooper Reel (Read These Out Loud for a Laugh)
  • “I had ate the evidence before anyone saw.” — nice try, but it's had eaten. The trophy was not, in fact, edible.
  • “She was already gone when I had arrived.” — close! Flip it: she had already gone when I arrived.

Text 3 · Assignment

Two Seats, One Empty

Read this one at home, before our next lesson.

When Deniz didn't show up for the class trip, everyone assumed the worst. She'd been quiet all week, and rumors started before the bus even left the parking lot. Some said she'd gotten in trouble. Others whispered that she and her best friend Mira had had a huge fight, and that Deniz had decided to skip the whole day out of spite.

Mira sat alone near the window, saying nothing. She'd already heard every theory twice, and none of them were true, but she hadn't corrected anyone — because the real reason felt too personal to explain to a bus full of gossiping classmates.

The truth came out at lunch. Deniz's grandmother, who had raised her since she was small, had been taken to the hospital that morning. Deniz had called Mira at six a.m., crying, and Mira had told her to stay home, that she'd explain everything later. Mira had kept the secret all day, not because she'd wanted to, but because Deniz had asked her to.

When the whispers finally reached Mira directly, she didn't explode. She just said, quietly, “You'd already decided what happened before you asked me. Next time, ask first.”

Nobody said anything for the rest of the bus ride.

Before our next lesson
  1. Read the story once for the story. Then read it again and underline every past perfect verb (“had” + past participle) you can find — there are at least ten.
  2. Think about your answer: was Mira right to keep the secret, or should she have explained sooner? Would you have done the same?
  3. Prepare a 60-second retell of the story from Deniz's point of view — we'll record it at the start of next lesson.

Teacher Facilitation Notes

Learner Profile

1:1 session, learner age 14, B1 Turkish-speaking student. Turkish has a direct structural parallel to English past perfect (-mış + -tı), a genuine advantage — lean on that bridge rather than re-teaching the concept from zero.

Vocabulary Sequencing

The 30-word bank is deliberately ordered easy–hard–easy–hard (clue→accuse→secret→suspicious→…) rather than sorted by level. This keeps momentum and confidence up without her noticing a difficulty ramp — do not relabel or resort the list in front of her.

Pain Points

The concept transfers easily from Turkish, but the two-word English structure and irregular participles (ate→eaten, went→gone) are the real friction — drill those, not the tense logic itself.

Keeping It Light

With one student, energy dips fast if it feels like a drill. Mascot asides, the Blooper Reel, and letting her "catch" your deliberately wrong sentences in Activity 2 Round 2 keep it playful without losing the grammar target. The Verdict discussion is the one place to slow down and let her think.
